Financial markets have an uncomfortable virtue: they put a price on almost everything, including
the probability that a regime will fall. When a major political event shakes a country, the assets
that depend on its solvency —sovereign bonds, *credit default swaps* (CDS), country-risk
indicators— reprice within minutes. This article uses the case of Venezuela as an excuse to learn
how to read that reaction and, along the way, to understand what some credit derivatives are and
how they behave.

> Note: the figures in this article are **approximate and illustrative**, meant to explain the
> mechanisms. They should not be taken as verified market data or as investment advice.

## Political and financial context

For years Venezuela has carried one of the most extreme credit situations in the world. After it
stopped paying its external debt in 2017, the country entered selective *default* on the bonds of
the Republic and of PDVSA, the state oil company. On top of that came financial sanctions that, in
practice, pulled these securities out of the usual trading circuits.

The result is a universe of bonds trading **well below their face value** —for much of the period,
somewhere between a few and a few tens of cents on the dollar— and a country risk that, measured by
EMBI-type spreads, sits at astronomical levels compared with any solvent issuer. In practice, the
price of a Venezuelan bond does not reflect "how much it yields", but rather **how much the market
expects to recover** if there is ever a restructuring.

Against that backdrop, a drastic political change is not noise: it is precisely the variable the
market has spent years trying to anticipate.

## Before (2025)

Throughout 2025, Venezuelan assets traded anchored to a *status quo* scenario:

- **Sovereign and PDVSA bonds**: at deeply distressed levels, consistent with the idea that any
  payment or swap was far off and uncertain. The price mostly embedded an expectation of future
  recovery (*recovery value*), not a coupon stream.
- **Country risk**: extraordinarily high spreads, typical of an issuer in *default*.
- **CDS**: here an important technical nuance is in order. A CDS is a contract that pays out if a
  "credit event" occurs (non-payment, restructuring…). In the Venezuelan case, that event **had
  already been declared years earlier**, so classic CDS on the debt had lost much of their original
  function: what remained was, above all, speculation on the recovery value.

The underlying reading in 2025 was one of **low and stable expectations**: little to gain while
nothing changed, much to reprice if something changed all at once.

## After (2026)

The news of the capture of Nicolás Maduro in January 2026 acts, in market terms, as an
**information shock**. Suddenly the scenario that seemed remote —political transition, a possible
gradual lifting of sanctions, eventual negotiation with creditors— gains probability. And the price
reacts before any concrete fact materializes:

- **Bonds**: a sharp rally. If the market shifts from pricing in, say, a very low recovery to a
  somewhat higher one, the price can **multiply** in percentage terms even while remaining far below
  face value. Going from ~10 to ~20 cents on the dollar is "only" ten cents, but it means doubling
  the investment: that is how non-linear the *distressed* world is.
- **Country risk**: spread compression. Not because Venezuela is suddenly solvent, but because the
  *probability of an orderly outcome* rises.
- **CDS / credit derivatives**: any live contract tied to recovery or to a new restructuring
  appreciates with the expectation. It is a reminder that a credit derivative does not pay for "what
  happens", but for **how the probability of what might happen changes**.

The pedagogical takeaway: the most violent move happens **on the day of the news**, not on the day
a real change materializes. Markets price expectations, and expectations reprice all at once.

## What about oil?

Venezuela is, above all, an oil story. Its capacity to service debt depends almost entirely on how
much crude it produces and at what price it sells it. That is why, alongside the bond reaction, it
is worth watching two things:

1. **Production and sanctions**: sanctions relief that allowed exports to restart and investment to
   return would change the economic base underpinning any *recovery value*. The bond market is, in a
   sense, putting a price on that option.
2. **The price of crude**: any expectation that more Venezuelan barrels return to the market has an
   effect, however modest, on the international price. That is the flip side: what is good news for
   creditors can be, at the margin, downward pressure on the barrel.

Put differently: in Venezuela, **the bond and the barrel tell the same story from two sides**.

## Conclusion

The episode works as a laboratory for three ideas that hold for any credit market:

- The price of a *distressed* bond is a **bet on recovery**, not a calculation of coupons.
- **Credit derivatives** transmit expectations: they move with the probability of the event, not
  only with the event.
- In heavily punished assets, reactions are **non-linear**: small changes in cents imply large
  changes in return.

Looking at Venezuela "through the market" does not replace political analysis, but it complements
it: prices summarize, in a single number, what thousands of participants believe is going to happen.

## Sources

For rigorous monitoring of a case like this, it helps to rely on data and analysis sources such as:

- Quotes for sovereign and corporate bonds (terminals such as Bloomberg or Refinitiv).
- Country-risk spreads, e.g. the **J.P. Morgan EMBI / EMBIG** indices.
- CDS data and credit events (determinations by the **ISDA Credit Derivatives Determinations
  Committee**).
- Crude production and export statistics (**OPEC**, energy agencies, sector reports).
- Coverage and analysis from specialized financial press.
